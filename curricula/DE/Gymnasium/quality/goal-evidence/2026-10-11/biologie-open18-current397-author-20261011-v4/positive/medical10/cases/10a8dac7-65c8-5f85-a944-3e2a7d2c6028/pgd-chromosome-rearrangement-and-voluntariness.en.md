# pgd-chromosome-rearrangement-and-voluntariness

Entirely fictional authored teaching case; no actual clinical execution, legal advice, learner observation or additional learner task quota.

Fictional case: Testing suitable cells from an embryo before transfer addresses a known familial chromosome rearrangement. Sampling, evaluation and counseling precede transfer choice. The technique can fail and addresses only the specified chromosome question. Two statements are supplied: A “Information can support our own choice,” and B “Not using the option is irresponsible.”  Fictional classroom ruleR, not a statement of current law: Under the model, testing before embryo transfer is permitted only after understandable counseling, freely given explicit consent and prior positive assessment by bodyG. Here counseling and free consent are present, but G’s assessment is unresolved. A favorable test result does not replace that assessment.

Explain method and timing. Analyze A and B by information, voluntariness, protective interests and social pressure. State a question to resolve before deciding. Apply supplied ruleR to the case and distinguish technical possibility, rule-based permission and ethical judgment. Which step remains unresolved before testing?

Fresh variation: The test fails technically. What is known, and why is portraying it as a normal result problematic? In addition, G has now issued a positive assessment, but the person withdraws consent before testing. Does that satisfy all conditions under R?

## Model response and scoring

Information comes from the sample before transfer; the examination does not repair the rearrangement. A can express nondirective information. B assumes a moral duty and may pressure voluntary choice. Embryo protection and the couple’s wishes may receive different weight; technical possibility does not resolve that conflict. Reliability, conditions or handling of findings are unresolved questions. Under ruleR, counseling and consent are insufficient: positive assessment by G is still missing. Testing may be technically possible but the described state does not satisfy all modeled permission conditions. Even a later positive assessment would not determine ethical agreement; reasoned weighing of values remains a separate question.

Transfer: An uninterpretable result is not a negative finding for the rearrangement. Methodological uncertainty remains. False reassurance removes the factual basis for informed choice. Under the changed rule premise the assessment condition is met, but free consent is no longer present. Thus testing still is not permitted under R. Counseling, assessment and technical findings cannot replace current consent. This limits the rule-bound course of action without substituting for an independent ethical rationale.

Scoring criteria: Explain principle, timing, limits and at least two competing ethical aspects. The changed technical premise must alter the information basis; claim neither permission nor actual execution. Apply all three conditions of the supplied fictional rule and withdrawn consent; do not require claims about current German law.
