# screening-to-targeted-prenatal-testing

Authored teaching case using entirely fictional data. No learner observation, actual execution, clinical or legal recommendation. Cases illustrate possible evidence; they do not impose an additional learner task quota.

Didactic method comparison: A blood test during pregnancy examines cell-free DNA; its pregnancy-associated component predominantly originates from the placenta. It provides an increased indication of a selected chromosome difference, not a final diagnosis. An alternative uses a fetal cell sample obtained after appropriate information for targeted chromosome analysis. Sequence analysis instead addresses a specifically suspected gene variant. Burdens and access remain prerequisites to clarify in this model.

Explain the sample-to-claim steps and distinguish screening indication, chromosome diagnosis and molecular sequence questions. Do not give a testing recommendation for a real person.

Fresh variation: There is no screening indication, but a particular familial gene variant is suspected. Does the previous screening resolve the question?

## Model response and scoring

Cell-free DNA in blood is analyzed to provide an indication of the specified chromosome difference. The indication is not yet a directly established fetal diagnosis. Targeted chromosome analysis of a suitable cell sample addresses chromosome count. Targeted sequencing addresses a specified base variant. Suitability depends on question, sample and stated limitations.

Transfer: No. Screening for selected chromosome differences does not automatically test the familial variant. The new question requires a suitable targeted molecular examination; a normal screening finding does not exclude that variant.

Scoring criteria: Connect sample and analysis, explicitly distinguish screening from diagnosis and sequence from chromosome questions, and justify the new question. A pregnancy blood test must not be described as a general genetic diagnosis.
