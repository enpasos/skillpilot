### Task 1

An alcohol-O lone pair attacks the NCO carbon; the C=N π pair moves to N. A suitable formal intermediate is R–N−–C(=O)–O+(H)–R′. B: accepts the O-bound H while the O–H electrons stay on O; N− accepts H from BH+, with B–H electrons regenerating B:. Product: R–NH–C(=O)–O–R′. Total charge and atoms are retained. One segment is O=C=N–(CH2)6–NH–C(=O)–O–CH2CH2–O–C(=O)–NH–(CH2)6–N=C=O; its NCO ends can react with more diol. Bifunctionality permits repetition without a small eliminated product. Ethanol can consume an NCO end but supplies no second OH group for further linking: end limitation despite the same initial OH count. This is a formal teaching model, not a claim about the dominant elementary route.

Scope limit: This is a formal charge-balanced teaching model, not evidence of a unique dominant elementary pathway. Equal OH:NCO group counts do not guarantee long chains when ethanol is monofunctional.

### Task 2

The polarity justifies alcohol-O pair attack at electrophilic C, with C=N π electrons moving to N. Via Ar–N−–C(=O)–O+(H)–R′, O deprotonation by B and N protonation by BH+, Ar–NH–C(=O)–O–R′ forms. Two such links connect, for example, diisocyanate–diol–diisocyanate, retaining outer NCO ends. No small molecule is eliminated in this dry group addition. N attack and water elimination are therefore incorrect; a product equation alone does not show a mechanism. Ethanol caps both NCO groups into an end-limited bisurethane, without diol bridges into long chains. Equal group counts cannot replace bifunctionality. Substituents can affect reactivity, but the material supplies no quantitative rate ranking.
