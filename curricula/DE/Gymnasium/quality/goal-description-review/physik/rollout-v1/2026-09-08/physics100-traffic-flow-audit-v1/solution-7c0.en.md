1. Range without air resistance and with equal launch and landing heights:
$R = \frac{v_0^2\sin(2\alpha)}{g}$.

Variant A: $R_A=196\sin(60^\circ)/9.81\approx17.3\,\mathrm{m}$.
Variant B: $R_B=196\sin(80^\circ)/9.81\approx19.7\,\mathrm{m}$.
At equal launch speed, B is preferable because $\sin(80^\circ)>\sin(60^\circ)$. This statement holds under the model assumptions, not for arbitrary launch conditions.

2. $70\,\mathrm{km/h}=19.44\,\mathrm{m/s}$.
Reaction distance: $s_R=vt_R\approx17.5\,\mathrm{m}$.
Braking distance: $s_B=v^2/(2a_B)\approx27.0\,\mathrm{m}$.
Total stopping distance: $s_A=s_R+s_B\approx44.5\,\mathrm{m}$.
The initial 40 m to the stationary obstacle is insufficient to stop before it in this model: the required stopping distance is about 4.5 m longer. This is not a general following-distance rule for two moving vehicles; their respective motions would have to be considered separately.

3. For the same momentum change, the magnitudes satisfy $|\overline F|=m|\Delta v|/\Delta t$.
Variant 1: $|\overline F_1|=75\cdot13.0/0.05=19500\,\mathrm{N}$.
Variant 2: $|\overline F_2|=75\cdot13.0/0.15=6500\,\mathrm{N}$.
Tripling the stopping time reduces the magnitude of the average force to one third, although the impulse magnitude remains $975\,\mathrm{Ns}$. This explains the modelled protective effect of deformation. The averages alone establish neither peak forces nor a specific injury prognosis.

4. Example improvements:
- Sports: Choose a more suitable launch angle under the given model conditions; for equal launch speed, maximum range occurs at $45^\circ$. A justified improvement in launch speed is also possible.
- Traffic: Choose a greater initial distance from the obstacle or a lower speed to make stopping in time possible. During an unavoidable impact, longer deformation time can reduce the average force for the same momentum change; it does not shorten the preceding total stopping distance. One physically defensible suggestion per context is sufficient.
